# Story: Proving It's Not Just Paper

**Theme**: Evidence & Audit Readiness

**Date Captured**: 2026-09-28

**Status**: Raw

---

## The Assumption

We assumed that once the documentation set was written, reviewed, and approved, the hard part was done. Policies signed, procedures in the Final folder, version numbers on the cover page. That felt like evidence enough.

---

## The Reality

The question that actually stopped us was a simple one: how do we show an auditor, or honestly just ourselves, that this isn't just another piece of paper?

Sitting with that question, the honest answer was: not yet, not well enough. A version number on a document proves someone edited a file. It doesn't prove the control described in it actually runs. An approved policy and an operating control are not the same claim, and we'd been treating them as if they were.

Three things came out of thinking it through properly:

1. **Version history has to be a real change log**, not just "v1.0 approved." Who changed what, and why, needs to be visible, the same discipline we already apply to the Draft/Final split for storage and access.
2. **KPIs only mean something as a trend**, not a single number. "100% of policies approved" proves nothing. Overdue risk treatment actions, time-to-remediate on findings, access reviews completed on schedule, tracked across several months, prove the system is actually moving.
3. **Certification bodies expect an operating history before Stage 2** (commonly around three months), specifically so there's a real cycle of evidence to sample, not a system switched on the week before the audit. We hadn't planned our timeline around that at all.

---

## Why It Matters

- An auditor samples evidence at random. If the only thing behind a control is the policy that describes it, that control fails the moment it's tested.
- A document register with a real change log is harder to fake retroactively than a Word doc's "last modified" field, and cheap to set up now versus reconstructing history later.
- Metrics captured once, right before an audit, read exactly like the paperwork problem we were trying to avoid. The same metrics tracked monthly, even showing mixed results, read as a system that's actually running.
- Certification requires a minimum operating period before Stage 2. If that's not built into the project timeline from the start, it becomes a scramble (or a delay) discovered too late to fix cheaply.

---

## The Decision

1. Extend the existing document register (already tracking version, owner, and approval) to require a change summary on every revision, not just a version bump.
2. Define a short list of KPIs tied to the actual risk treatment plan (not vanity metrics like "policies approved"), and start tracking them monthly starting now, well before any audit is scheduled.
3. Build a minimum operating period into the project timeline explicitly, rather than treating certification readiness as "documentation complete."
4. Plan for at least one internal audit cycle and one management review to happen and be evidenced before Stage 2, not scheduled for the same week as it.

---

## The Lesson

**A completed document set feels like progress. An auditor (and reality) measures something else: can you point at a log entry, a ticket, or a version diff and say "here's it happening," instead of pointing at the policy that says it should happen.**

Standard ISO guidance tells you to produce evidence. It doesn't tell you that evidence has to be a trend across time to be credible, or that certification bodies expect months of operating history before they'll even look at it. Both of those need to be planned for from early in the project, not discovered when the documentation is already "finished."

**Next time**: Don't let "the documents are approved" stand in for "the ISMS is credible." Ask the harder question (how would we prove this operates, to someone skeptical) early enough that there's still time to build the evidence trail, not just the paperwork.

---

## Time Investment

~1 hour of focused discussion to surface the gap and land on the four decisions above. The real cost isn't in this hour, it's in however many months of tracked KPIs and operating history now need to happen before Stage 2, which wasn't previously budgeted into the timeline at all.
