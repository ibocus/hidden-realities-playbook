# Story: Which Language Do We Write This In?

**Theme**: Format Harmonization

**Date Captured**: 2026-08-11

**Status**: Raw

---

## The Assumption

We assumed language wouldn't really be a decision. Write the ISMS documentation, roll it out, done. If anything, we figured whatever the organization already used informally would just carry over.

---

## The Reality

Going through existing process and procedure documents (not ISO documents yet, just the general operational ones already in place), we found no consistency at all. Some were in English. Some were in French. No pattern to which was which, no policy behind the split, just years of whoever wrote a given document picking whatever language they were comfortable in.

That forced the question before we'd even started drafting ISO documentation: what language do *we* write in?

We settled on English as the base language. But it's not that simple. Depending on the type and purpose of a document, and who it's actually meant to reach, a French version might still be needed. A policy aimed at leadership is a different audience than a procedure a general employee is expected to follow day to day. We're still early, so exactly where that line gets drawn (which documents get a French version, which don't) isn't fully decided yet.

One thing that's already clear: translation isn't free. Time and fees for translating documents are a real cost that needs tracking, not an afterthought.

---

## Why It Matters

- Language isn't neutral. If a security procedure is only in a language part of the workforce isn't fully comfortable in, the control exists on paper but not in practice.
- Auditors expect evidence that's understood, not just documented. A policy nobody can actually read properly is a weak control.
- Mixing languages inconsistently across documents (some English, some French, no rule) creates confusion about which is authoritative and makes maintenance harder.
- Translation has real cost implications (time and fees) that typically aren't budgeted for in an ISMS project plan.
- Once you commit to a default language, retrofitting exceptions later is more expensive than deciding the criteria upfront.

---

## The Decision

Still in progress. So far:

1. English selected as the default/base language for ISO documentation.
2. Left open that specific documents may need a French version, decided case by case based on the document's audience and purpose (e.g. leadership-facing vs. general-employee-facing).
3. Flagged translation time and fees as a cost to actively track going forward, rather than something absorbed silently into the project timeline.
4. Not yet decided: the actual rule for *which* documents get translated and which don't. That's the open question.

---

## The Lesson

**Standard ISO guidance doesn't tell you what language to write in.** It assumes you already know, or that it doesn't matter. In an international or multilingual organization, it very much matters, and the standard offers zero help deciding.

If your organization has any history of documentation in more than one language, this decision doesn't wait for you to be ready. It surfaces the moment you start reviewing what already exists.

**What to consider before you're forced into it:**
- Decide the base language deliberately, tied to who the primary audience of record actually is (regulators, auditors, leadership) rather than defaulting to whatever's convenient.
- Segment by audience early: are documents for leadership, for general staff, or both? That answer should drive which get translated.
- Whatever you land on, apply it consistently. A document existing in English for one department and French for another, with no rule behind it, is worse than picking either language and sticking to it.
- Budget translation time and fees as a real line item, not something you discover mid-project.
