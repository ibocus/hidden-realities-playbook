# Story: Before You Touch a Single Control

**Theme**: Stakeholder Engagement

**Date Captured**: 2026-08-17

**Status**: Raw

---

## The Assumption

We assumed scope was mostly an internal, technical call. The ISMS team drafts what's in and out, management gives a general nod, and the real work starts.

---

## The Reality

It doesn't work that way. Before any ISO 27001 implementation work begins, the scope document has to be formally signed and acknowledged by the sponsor or senior management, not just informally approved or mentioned in a meeting.

It's not a document you settle once and file away either. It's the very first thing an auditor asks for when an audit starts. Every control, every risk assessment, every piece of evidence after that gets measured against what that document actually says.

Which means it can't just reflect whatever is technically convenient to certify. What's written in the scope document has to genuinely match what the sponsor thinks they're getting. If those two things drift apart, and it gets discovered by the auditor instead of internally, that's a far worse conversation to have.

---

## Why It Matters

- Auditors start with this document. A weak or misaligned scope statement undermines everything that follows.
- Sponsors sign off on what they believe the scope covers. If the written scope doesn't match their actual expectation, it surfaces at the worst possible time, mid-audit, or when something they assumed was covered turns out not to be.
- Scope, once signed, defines what's in and out of the ISMS. Get it wrong and you're either certifying too little (sponsor unhappy) or committing to controls over more than intended (extra cost, extra effort).
- It's a governance document as much as a technical one. It needs to be written in language the sponsor actually understands and agrees with, not just security-team shorthand.

---

## The Decision

Treat the scope document as a formal sign-off gate, not a formality:

1. Draft the scope in plain terms the sponsor can actually evaluate, not just security/audit jargon.
2. Walk the sponsor through it directly before asking for signature, checking it matches what they expect the ISMS to cover.
3. Get it formally signed and acknowledged by the sponsor or senior management before any implementation work starts.

---

## The Lesson

Scope isn't something you write once internally and move past. It's the foundation everything else rests on.

Get the sponsor's actual sign-off, not just a nod, and make sure what's written genuinely matches what they expect, before any control work begins. The first person to test whether the signed document says what everyone assumed is usually the auditor, not you. Better to find any mismatch before that.
